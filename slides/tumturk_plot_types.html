Measuring the Impact of Plot Types on Physical Change

A Diachronic Analysis of Urban Form Evolution in New York, Melbourne, and Barcelona

Onur Tümtürk, Justyna Karakiewicz, Fjalar J. de Haan

The University of Melbourne

Introduction & Research Gap

Urban Form as Active Determinant: Urban form constrains or facilitates how cities adapt to environmental and social disturbances.

Understudied Element: Plots are acknowledged as fundamental morphological units, yet remain the least studied aspect of urban form.

Methodological Gap

  • Historical studies are qualitative and rely on isolated geometric measures (size, shape).
  • 95% of recent quantitative studies are synchronic (single timeframe).

Research Aim

Investigate the diachronic relationship between multi-variable plot typologies and patterns of physical building change across nearly two centuries.

Theoretical Background & Hypotheses

Geometric vs. Configurational Approaches

  • Geometric: Measures individual plot properties in isolation (e.g., plot area, individual frontage).
  • Configurational: Measures the relational properties of plot structures within an accessible catchment (e.g., 400m walking reach).

Key Morphological Hypothesis

  • Fine-grained, narrow, compact plot patterns foster distributed control among diverse actors, increasing transaction costs/efforts for parcel amalgamation and resulting in higher resistance to physical building replacement.
  • Coarse-grained, broad plot patterns lower transformation friction, accelerating building demolition and replacement.

Case Studies & Data Framework

Comparative Case Studies (2.5 km² central grids + 400m buffer)

  1. New York (Midtown): Established grid with intense vertical & horizontal transformation.
  2. Melbourne (CBD): Large block structure with rapid coarsening over time.
  3. Barcelona (Eixample): Octagonal block structures with chamfered corners.

Temporal Boundaries (~40–50 year intervals)

  • Timeframes: Late 1800s (\(T_1\)), Early 1900s (\(T_2\)), Mid-1900s (\(T_3\)), 2000s (\(T_4\)).
  • Data Sources: Georeferenced historical maps, cadastral plans, and digital databases vectorized in QGIS.

Methodological Workflow

  1. Vectorize Historical GIS Data
  2. Compute Configurational Plot Variables (400m Reach)
  3. \(k\)-means Cluster Analysis
  4. Pearson’s Chi-Square Test

Configurational Variables Defined

Metric Code Morphological Meaning
Accessible Plot Density \(\text{APD}\) Total number of accessible plots within 400m reach. Captures urban grain fineness.
Accessible Plot Frontage \(\text{APF}\) Cumulative street frontage length divided by cumulative plot perimeter. Captures narrowness vs. broadness.
Accessible Plot Compactness \(\text{APC}\) Cumulative plot area divided by cumulative minimum bounding rectangle area. Captures shape regularity.

\[\text{Physical Change} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{Building Demolition or Construction} \\ 0 & \text{Building Persistence} \end{cases}\]

The Four Plot Typologies

Clusters derived from \(k\)-means analysis across all case studies:

  1. PT1 (Medium-Grain / Medium-Front / Medium-Compact):
    • Average accessibility, deeper plots, relatively irregular subdivision shapes.
  2. PT2 (Medium-Grain / Medium-Front / Compact):
    • Transitional type; higher accessibility than PT1, regular and highly compact.
  3. PT3 (Fine-Grain / Narrow-Front / Compact):
    • Highest plot accessibility, narrow street frontages, small regular plots (Fine-grain par excellence).
  4. PT4 (Coarse-Grain / Broad-Front / Medium-Compact):
    • Lowest plot density, expansive street frontages, often occupying full/half urban blocks.

Evolution Across Time and Space

New York (Midtown)

  • Transitioned from PT3 dominant (88.2% in \(T_1\)) to a mix dominated by PT2 and coarse-grained PT4 (26.2% by \(T_4\)).

Melbourne (CBD)

  • Rapid transformation from PT2/PT1 towards coarse-grained PT4 (82.9% by \(T_3\)).
  • By \(T_4\), transformed into a homogenous coarse-grained plot structure (100% PT4).

Barcelona (Eixample)

  • Remarkably persistent: Maintained over 99% PT1 across all four time periods. Physical changes were accommodated within existing plot boundaries without parcel amalgamation.

Key Finding: Plot Types vs. Physical Change

A Pearson’s chi-square test of independence confirmed a statistically significant relationship (\(\chi^2 = 360.57, p < 0.001\)).

Core Conclusion:

Coarse-grained, broad plot configurations (PT4) are nearly 50% more susceptible to physical building demolition and replacement compared to fine-grained, narrow configurations (PT3).

Discussion & Theoretical Contributions

Relational Character Matters:

  • Building persistence is governed by the character of surrounding plot structures, not just individual plot dimensions in isolation.

Mechanisms of Resistance:

  • Fine-grained subdivisions distribute control among many owners.
  • Amalgamating fine plots requires higher financial, legal, and organizational effort, constraining rapid redevelopment.

Urban Form Resilience:

  • Fine-grained patterns spread risk and support incremental, adaptive evolution, whereas coarse-grained patterns favor large-scale replacement.

Implications for Design & Planning

Preservation Strategies:

  • To protect urban character and heritage, policies should enforce or preserve fine-grained land subdivisions (PT3).

Targeted Urban Renewal:

  • Where high density or rapid transformation is desired, establishing or facilitating broader/coarser plot subdivisions (PT4) lowers friction for development.

Re-establishing the “Art of Subdivision”:

  • Strategic subdivision rules can balance character preservation with dynamic adaptability.

Limitations & Future Directions

Spatial Constraints:

  • Focused on central grid areas; future studies should examine suburban, historical medieval, and informal contexts.

Variable Expansion:

  • Incorporate metrics measuring plot heterogeneity/diversity within accessible reach.

Multi-scalar Analysis:

  • Test configurational plot access across multiple catchment radii (e.g., 200m, 800m, 1200m).

Conclusion

Plots as Frameworks:

  • Plot patterns act as active structural frameworks that guide long-term urban evolution.

Empirical Validation:

  • Provided quantitative, multi-city, diachronic evidence linking plot structures to physical change over two centuries.

Evidence-Based Planning:

  • Offers actionable morphometric metrics to inform land subdivision policy and urban resilience planning.
